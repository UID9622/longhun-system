## What Open Source Means — And What It Doesn't

Open source is not just a license. It is a **philosophical position** about the relationship between creators and users of software. The Longhun System's open source commitment is not marketing. It is not a "community growth strategy." It is an expression of our foundational belief: **technology that governs people's lives must be auditable by the people it governs.**

But let us be clear about what our open source means. The open source movement has been co-opted by corporations that release code publicly while retaining all practical power. We reject this hollow form.

---

## Our Open Source Principles

### 1. Source Available, Always
Every component that touches user data, makes governance decisions, or enforces rules must have publicly available source code. Not "core modules only." Not "the interesting parts." Everything that matters.

### 2. Not Just Code — Infrastructure
Open source means more than code. It means:
- Documentation anyone can understand
- Build processes anyone can reproduce
- Test suites anyone can run
- Architecture decisions anyone can trace

### 3. Audit, Not Just Access
Source code access that doesn't enable meaningful audit is performative. We commit to:
- Clear dependency chains
- Documented design decisions
- Traceable DNA markers on every significant change
- Independent audit report publication

### 4. No Open-Core Bait-and-Switch
We reject the "open core, proprietary enterprise" model. If a feature governs users, its source must be open. Full stop. We will not release a crippled open version while keeping governance logic proprietary.

### 5. Fork Freedom
Anyone may fork the Longhun System. Fork and improve. Fork and adapt. Fork and compete. The only condition: retain attribution to the original creators and the DNA traceability chain.

---

## What We Don't Open

Some things cannot and should not be open:

- **Private keys, signing keys, cryptographic seeds** — These are the sovereign root of trust. Opening them would destroy security. They live in hardware-isolated environments.

- **User data** — User data is not ours to open. It belongs to users. We will never "open source" anyone's personal information.

- **Security vulnerability details** — During active remediation, vulnerability specifics stay private. After patching, full disclosure with timeline.

Being open doesn't mean being naked. It means being **honest about what's closed and why.**

---

## Why Source-Available, Not Just Open Source

The OSI definition of "open source" has value. But it is not sufficient for governance software.

A governance system that is merely "OSI-compliant open source" but whose actual running instances are opaque, whose decision-making processes are hidden, and whose audit trails are private — is not truly open, regardless of its license.

We add: **source-available + auditable + decision-transparent + jurisdiction-bound**.

## The Chinese Context

Our open source commitment is made within Chinese legal jurisdiction. We comply with Chinese laws regarding software publication, cryptography, and data security. We reject any interpretation of "open source" that would require us to violate Chinese law or compromise Chinese national security.

This is not a contradiction. It is the recognition that "openness" must be defined within sovereignty, not outside it. Just as a country can have open borders for travel while maintaining border security, the Longhun System can be open for audit while maintaining sovereign boundaries.

## To the Global Open Source Community

We respect your work. Much of the software stack we depend on — Linux, Python, Git, countless libraries — was built by the global open source community.

We contribute back in the ways we can:
- Publishing protocols and architectures for others to learn from
- Maintaining clear, documented code
- Attribution to all upstream projects we depend on

But we will not be shamed into compromising our principles. Chinese digital sovereignty is not incompatible with open source. It is the foundation upon which meaningful Chinese open source can be built.

---

## A Call to Other Builders

If you are building infrastructure that governs people's digital lives:

1. **Open your source.** Not the marketing page. The actual governance logic.
2. **Publish your audits.** Not just "we passed security review." The actual findings.
3. **Document your decisions.** Why did you choose this approach over that one? Write it down.
4. **Accept forks.** If someone can do it better, let them. Competition improves infrastructure.
5. **Remember who you serve.** Your code governs people. They have a right to see it.
